I was reading a Facebook thread the other week — someone had shared something about India's lunar mission, or its payments infrastructure, one of those things India has been quietly building while most of the world looks elsewhere. The comments were mostly what you'd expect. And then, with a kind of clockwork reliability, one kept appearing.

*But there are slums in India.*

Not once. The same person, returning to it across several replies, deploying it each time like a trump card. Someone would raise something India had done, and she'd come back with the slums. It was less an argument than a verdict.

I've been sitting with that comment ever since. Not because it's wrong — it isn't — but because of the particular work it's doing. The "but" is the thing. It positions poverty as a rebuttal. As if a country can only hold one truth at a time.

---

The slums are real. I want to be clear about that, because this isn't a piece about defending India from uncomfortable facts. Somewhere around forty percent of India's urban population lives in informal settlements. In Mumbai — one of the most economically energetic cities on earth — more than half the population lives in areas that would be classified as slums by any standard measure. These are real people, real conditions, a real and serious failure of housing and infrastructure at scale.

But here's where it gets more complicated than the Facebook comment allows. The instinct, looking at this from the outside, is to think: why doesn't someone just fix it? Clear it up. Build proper housing. Move people in. India has, in fact, tried versions of this. What those efforts have repeatedly produced is something that looks like improvement on paper and is devastating in practice — families given days of notice, homes demolished while belongings are still inside, people relocated to the far edges of cities, away from the informal economies and job networks and community structures that were the entire reason they came to the city in the first place. The Indian Supreme Court ruled as far back as 1985 that eviction without due notice, rehabilitation, and consideration of livelihoods is both legally and morally indefensible. That ruling has not stopped evictions. What it has made clear is that this is not a problem with a simple solution — because the people living in these settlements are not problems. They are people, with lives that don't pause for a bulldozer.

There's also a story here about scale that gets lost in the framing. India's urban population grew fourteen times over in the space of a century. Cities built for one kind of life absorbed millions arriving from the countryside, looking for something better, or just something possible. The informal settlements that formed weren't a symptom of neglect alone. They were adaptation. In many cases they sit close to employment hubs — not by accident, but because proximity to work is exactly the point.

---

None of that cancels the problem. But I keep coming back to why this particular problem is the one that gets deployed as a full stop.

I've spent enough time in comment sections to notice a pattern. The poverty of a Western country is treated as a challenge it's working on. A gap between where it is and where it's heading. Nobody drops into a conversation about the NHS to say *but there are food banks in Britain*. Nobody interrupts a discussion of Silicon Valley with *but there are tent cities in San Francisco*. Those realities exist — and they're serious — but they're not used as reasons to disqualify everything else.

With India, it works differently. The slums don't sit alongside the story. They become the story. Everything else — the space programme, the digital payments network that now handles nearly half of all real-time payment transactions globally, the pharmaceutical manufacturing, the film industry, the classical traditions, the literature — gets filed behind the "but." Not as part of a complicated picture. As things that don't quite count until the other thing is resolved.

I don't think the woman in that thread was being cruel. I think she genuinely believed she was keeping the conversation honest. There's a version of that impulse that comes from real care — a desire not to let inconvenient realities get smoothed over by good news. I recognise it. I feel it myself sometimes when the cheerleading gets too loud.

But there's a difference between holding complexity and using one truth to cancel another. The question isn't whether slums exist. It's what kind of attention we're paying, and to what end.

---

What I keep coming back to is this: India doesn't translate easily. It's too big, too contradictory, too much of several things at once. A country that can put a spacecraft on the south pole of the moon and still have hundreds of millions of people living without reliable sanitation — that's not a paradox to be resolved. It's just what a country of 1.4 billion people, in the middle of one of the fastest economic transformations in human history, actually looks like.

The single image — the slum, the poverty, the chaos — is easier to hold than that. I understand why people reach for it.

I just wonder sometimes what it would look like to resist the reach. To stay with the discomfort of the whole picture a little longer, and see what you find there.
