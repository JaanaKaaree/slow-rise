*The Slow Rise — Essay*

---

I came across a statistic recently that I've been unable to leave alone. A single Mughal miniature painting — small enough to hold in one hand, made with a brush sometimes containing just a single hair — sold at a London auction for over a hundred thousand pounds. The buyer was a European collector. The artist has been dead for four centuries. The painting itself depicts a court scene no bigger than a paperback novel.

What got me wasn't the price. It was where the painting ended up.

Not in a museum in Jaipur or Delhi. Not in a private collection in Mumbai. In London. And this isn't unusual. The finest surviving examples of Indian miniature painting — the Hamzanama, the Akbarnama, work by Bishandas and Basawan, the great Rajput and Pahari masters — are spread across the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Freer Gallery in Washington, the Bibliothèque nationale in Paris. Walk through these galleries and you're watching one civilisation's intimate art beheld by another civilisation's tourists.

There's something in that geography worth sitting with.

---

The tradition is older than most people realise. It starts not in the Mughal courts but on palm leaves, in Bengal, in the 8th century — Buddhist monks illustrating manuscripts with a patience that had nothing to do with decoration and everything to do with devotion. By the time paper arrived in India in the 12th century, the form had already developed a discipline, a grammar, a sense of what small could hold.

Then the Mughals arrived, and something extraordinary happened. Akbar established a royal atelier of over a hundred painters. He brought Persian masters in and set them alongside Indian artists from Gujarat, Gwalior, Kashmiri. What came out wasn't Persian and wasn't quite Indian. It was a new thing — a synthesis so refined it took fifteen years just to illustrate one manuscript, the Hamzanama, which ran to over fourteen hundred individual paintings.

The artists whose names survived — Basawan, Bishandas, Manohar, Sahibdin of the Mewar school — were working at a level of technical mastery that left European visitors to the Mughal court openly bewildered. Jahangir famously praised Bishandas as unrivalled in portraiture. The emperor himself was a serious critic, not just a patron. He could identify which section of a collaborative painting had been done by which hand.

That kind of patronage produced a kind of art. When the patronage disappeared — when the Mughals fell, when the British arrived and dismantled the court economies that sustained these ateliers — the tradition didn't die, but it became untethered. The chitrakars, the painter families who had passed these skills through generations, found themselves without the networks that had made their work matter.

---

Here is what I find interesting about where we are now.

The form survived. Not just in museums — though it's in plenty of those — but in studios, in families, in art schools. In Jaipur and Udaipur and Kangra, there are painters who are direct descendants of those lineages, still working with natural stone pigments and squirrel-hair brushes, still layering wasli paper in the traditional way. A seventh-generation painter in Jaipur recently told a journalist: "We paint what our ancestors painted. But today we also teach workshops and travel internationally to festivals."

That sentence is doing a lot of work. It's the sound of something figuring out how to stay alive in a world that wasn't designed for it.

And then there's the contemporary side, which is stranger and more exciting than the revival story alone. Artists like Manisha Gera Baswani are working in the miniature tradition but bringing contemporary themes in — personal narrative, urban life, questions about identity and belonging. The form is proving more elastic than it looks. Those precise borders and flat registers that once contained court scenes and divine love stories can apparently hold almost anything you put inside them.

What the miniature has — what it has always had — is an argument about attention. It's not a form that rewards a glance. It insists you come close. It insists you stay. The detail in a single Kangra painting, the way a monsoon cloud is rendered in the Rasikapriya, the botanical precision in Jahangir's natural history commissions — none of it lands at arm's length. You have to want to see it.

That's an unfashionable ask in 2026. And maybe that's exactly why it's interesting.

---

The revival story, if there is one, isn't a single story. It's several things happening at once — traditional craftsmen keeping lineages alive, contemporary artists reanimating the form for their own purposes, global collectors suddenly paying serious money for work that serious Indian institutions largely ignored, diaspora communities rediscovering something they had vaguely known about but never looked at closely.

What's missing, or what feels thin, is the connective tissue between these things. The Mughal miniatures in the V&A don't talk to the Jaipur studio painter. The contemporary artist reinterpreting the form for an international art fair audience isn't necessarily in conversation with the seventh-generation chitrakar three hours away. There's no shared story being told. There's a lot of individual survival.

I keep thinking about the squirrel-hair brush. The traditional ones use a specific hair, specific thickness, capable of holding a fine point that almost no synthetic material can replicate. The knowledge of how to make them, how to load them, how to use them — that knowledge lives in human hands. It doesn't transfer through books. It transfers through proximity, through years of watching, through failure and correction and patience.

That kind of knowledge has a way of disappearing quietly. Not with a bang. Just one generation that decided it wasn't worth the difficulty.

What I'm not sure about — what I don't think anyone is sure about — is whether the current moment of global interest arrives in time to change the calculation for that generation. Whether the young painter in a family of chitrakars looks at what's happening in London auction houses and in international galleries and decides that the difficulty is, after all, worth it.

I hope they do. But hope isn't a strategy, and this particular form has already been lost once before — rescued more by accident and colonial collecting than by any deliberate preservation. It would be something if this time, we actually chose it.
