# The Instrument Nobody Exports

I have been thinking about the sarangi lately.

Not because I heard one recently — I did not. It came up in a conversation about what India sends into the world and what it keeps to itself, almost by accident, and I have not been able to shake it since.

The sarangi is one of the most expressive bowed instruments ever built. It has somewhere between thirty-five and forty strings, most of which are never played directly but instead vibrate in sympathy with the ones that are. The result is a sound with extraordinary depth — not just a note, but a note with its own atmosphere around it. Musicians who have heard both say it can get closer to the human voice than almost anything else.

It is also almost completely unknown outside South Asia.

This is not because it is obscure within India. Classical Hindustani vocalists have relied on it for centuries. It was the accompanying instrument of choice before the harmonium arrived and complicated everything. There are masters of it alive today whose skill is, by any measure, world-class.

And yet if you asked a reasonably curious person in London or São Paulo or Auckland to name an Indian instrument, they would say sitar. Maybe tabla. Almost certainly not sarangi.

I keep coming back to why.

Part of the answer is practical. The sarangi is extraordinarily difficult to play and famously hard to keep in tune. It has no single standardised form — different regions have built it differently for centuries. This makes it resistant to the kind of codification that helps instruments travel. You cannot write a beginner method book for the sarangi the way you can for the guitar.

But I think there is something else underneath the practical explanation. India has, for most of its modern history, been ambivalent about the project of actively exporting its culture. It has shared what people came to find. It has not often gone looking for audiences.

The contrast with Korea is uncomfortable if you sit with it long enough. Korean soft power did not happen by accident. There were institutions, investment, a deliberate decision made somewhere that the culture was worth sending out into the world. The result is a generation of people in places that had no prior connection to Korea who now care about it — not because they were told to, but because something reached them.

India has things that could reach people. The sarangi is one of them — not despite its strangeness but because of it. A sound that close to the human voice, built from that many strings, carrying centuries of a particular way of understanding music — that is not a difficult thing to love. It just needs to be heard.

The question is who decides it is worth the effort of being heard.
